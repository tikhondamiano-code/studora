# Problem Definition: High School Study Planning & Follow-Through

**Document type:** Problem & User Statement
**Version:** 1.0 (Draft)
**Date:** November 25, 2026

---

## 1. Summary

High school students who want top grades often know *what* they need to study but struggle to turn that intent into a realistic plan and then follow it. Without a clear plan, studying is delayed until it feels urgent, preparation gets compressed into the last few days, and final exam results come in below the student's goal. This document defines the problem, the target user, and how we'll know if we've solved it.

---

## 2. Target User

### 2.1 Primary Persona: "Alex," the Ambitious Procrastinator

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age** | 15 |
| **Gender** | Male |
| **Grade level** | High school (9th–10th grade) |
| **Course load** | 5–7 classes, each with a cumulative final exam |
| **Goal** | Earn an A (90% or higher) in every class on finals |
| **Motivation** | Wants the grades; may be driven by personal pride, parent expectations, GPA, sports eligibility, or future college plans |
| **Core struggle** | Building a study plan and sticking to it |

### 2.2 Context and Environment

- **Competing demands:** Homework, extracurriculars, sports, part-time responsibilities, social life, and family obligations all compete for the same after-school hours.
- **Digital distractions:** Phone, social media, games, and video are constantly available and designed to hold attention.
- **Limited planning skills:** Has likely never been formally taught how to break a large goal (e.g., "ace the biology final") into daily, manageable tasks.
- **Developmental stage:** At 15, the brain's planning and impulse-control systems are still developing, so prioritizing a distant reward (a final grade weeks away) over an immediate one (a video game now) is genuinely harder than it is for adults.
- **Support system:** May have parents or teachers who remind him to study, but reminders alone don't provide structure.

### 2.3 What Alex Says (Representative Quotes — to be validated in research)

> "I know I should start studying, I just don't know where to start."
> "I made a schedule once, but I fell behind after two days and gave up on it."
> "I'll study tomorrow. I work better under pressure anyway."
> "There's so much material, it feels impossible, so I just don't look at it."

## 3. Problem Statement

### 3.1 Formal Problem Statement

> **A 15-year-old high school student who wants to earn A's (90%+) on all of his finals struggles to create a structured study plan and stick to it. Because he lacks a clear, manageable plan, he procrastinates and does little or no consistent studying. As a result, he earns final grades below 90%, falling short of his own goal.**

### 3.2 User Statement (Point-of-View Format)

> **Alex**, a motivated 15-year-old high school student,
> **needs** a way to turn the overwhelming task of preparing for finals into a clear, realistic plan he can actually follow day by day,
> **because** without that structure he puts off studying until it's too late, and ends up with grades below the A's he's working toward.

### 3.3 "How Might We" Questions

- How might we help Alex break down each final into small, specific study tasks?
- How might we make starting a study session feel easier than avoiding it?
- How might we help Alex recover when he falls behind, instead of abandoning the plan?
- How might we make progress toward an A feel visible and rewarding *before* the exam?
- How might we fit a study plan around Alex's real schedule (sports, homework, downtime)?

---

## 4. Problem Breakdown

The problem is a chain of three linked failures. Each one feeds the next.

```
[1] Can't build a plan  →  [2] Can't stick to a plan  →  [3] Procrastination  →  [4] Grade < 90%
```

### 4.1 Failure Point 1: Difficulty Creating a Study Plan

| Root Cause | Description |
|---|---|
| **Overwhelm** | A cumulative final covers months of material; it's hard to know where to begin. |
| **Unclear scope** | The student may not know exactly what will be on the test or which topics matter most. |
| **No method** | He hasn't been taught how to estimate time, prioritize subjects, or schedule backward from an exam date. |
| **Multiple exams at once** | Finals cluster within the same 1–2 weeks, making it hard to balance subjects. |
| **Unrealistic planning** | When he does plan, he may overestimate how much he can do per day, setting himself up to fail. |

### 4.2 Failure Point 2: Difficulty Sticking to the Plan

| Root Cause | Description |
|---|---|
| **Distant reward** | The payoff (a good grade) is weeks away; distractions offer instant rewards. |
| **No accountability** | Nobody checks whether he followed the plan on a given day. |
| **All-or-nothing thinking** | Missing one session makes the whole plan feel "broken," so he abandons it. |
| **Rigid plans** | Plans don't flex when life happens (practice runs late, a surprise assignment). |
| **Lack of visible progress** | He can't see that small sessions are adding up, so effort feels pointless. |
| **Low task clarity** | Plan items like "study chemistry" are too vague to start on immediately. |

### 4.3 Failure Point 3: Procrastination

Procrastination is the *behavior* that results from the first two failures. It is commonly driven by:
- **Avoidance of discomfort** (studying feels boring, hard, or anxiety-inducing)
- **Fear of failure** (not trying protects him from feeling like he tried and still failed)
- **False confidence in cramming** ("I'll do it all the night before")
- **Easy access to distractions**

### 4.4 Outcome: Unfavorable Grade

- **Definition of failure:** Any final grade **below 90%** (below an A).
- **Typical pattern:** Last-minute cramming, poor sleep before exams, shallow retention, and test anxiety lead to lower scores.

---

## 5. Impact

| Area | Impact on the Student |
|---|---|
| **Academic** | Lower final and semester grades; lower GPA. |
| **Emotional** | Stress, guilt, anxiety, and frustration; reduced confidence ("I'm just bad at studying"). |
| **Behavioral** | Reinforces the cycle: poor results lower motivation, which increases procrastination next time. |
| **Family** | Potential conflict with parents over grades and study habits. |
| **Future** | GPA affects honors placement, scholarships, college admissions, and sometimes sports eligibility. |

---

## 6. Current Alternatives & Why They Fall Short

| Current Approach | Why It Doesn't Fully Work |
|---|---|
| Paper planners / school agendas | Require self-discipline to use; no reminders or adaptation. |
| Generic to-do or calendar apps | Not built for exam prep; don't help break down material. |
| Parent reminders / nagging | Creates friction; provides pressure, not structure. |
| Teacher-provided study guides | Tell him *what* to study, not *when* or *how much*. |
| Tutoring | Effective but costly and not available to everyone. |
| Cramming | Feels productive but leads to poor retention and lower scores. |

---

## 7. Assumptions to Validate

These are beliefs built into this problem definition that should be tested through interviews or surveys with real students:

1. The student is genuinely motivated to get A's (not only pressured by others).
2. The main barrier is **planning and follow-through**, not understanding the material itself.
3. The student has access to a smartphone or computer.
4. The student has some free time each day that could be used for studying.
5. Procrastination is driven more by overwhelm and lack of structure than by lack of interest.
6. Small, consistent study sessions would raise his grades above 90% if he completed them.

---

## 8. Scope

### In Scope
- Creating a study plan for upcoming finals
- Staying consistent with that plan over days or weeks
- Reducing procrastination around exam preparation
- Tracking progress toward the 90%+ goal

### Out of Scope (for now)
- Teaching subject content (e.g., explaining math concepts)
- Diagnosing or treating learning differences (e.g., ADHD) or anxiety disorders
- Daily homework management outside of finals preparation
- Students in grades outside high school

---

## 9. Success Criteria

How we'll know the problem is being solved:

| Metric | Target (example — to be refined) |
|---|---|
| **Plan creation** | Student creates a complete finals study plan at least 2 weeks before exams. |
| **Plan adherence** | Student completes 75%+ of planned study sessions. |
| **Study start time** | Student begins studying for each final at least 10 days before the exam. |
| **Recovery rate** | After missing a session, student resumes the plan within 1–2 days. |
| **Outcome** | Student earns 90%+ on all (or more) of his finals. |
| **Self-reported stress** | Student reports lower stress and higher confidence going into finals. |

---

## 10. Open Questions for Research

1. What does the student's typical after-school day actually look like, hour by hour?
2. When he has tried to plan before, at what exact point did the plan break down?
3. What motivates him most: grades themselves, parents, rewards, competition, or something else?
4. Which subjects does he procrastinate on the most, and why?
5. What study methods does he currently use (re-reading, flashcards, practice tests)?
6. What role do parents, friends, or teachers currently play in his studying?
7. What tools (apps, planners) has he tried, and why did he stop using them?

---

## 11. Next Steps

1. **Validate the problem:** Interview 5–10 students who match the persona.
2. **Refine the persona:** Update Section 2 based on real quotes and behaviors.
3. **Prioritize root causes:** Identify which failure point (planning vs. follow-through) has the biggest impact.
4. **Ideate solutions:** Use the "How Might We" questions in Section 3.3 to brainstorm.
5. **Define a measurable pilot:** Test a solution during one finals period and measure against Section 9.
